\section{Retrospective adjustments and offline segmentation}\label{app:retro-extra}

\paragraph{Further retrospective adjustments.} We fitted eight more retrospective seasonal adjustments in exploratory work, among them a Fourier regression, Prophet \citep{taylor2018} and TBATS \citep{delivera2011}. We scored their output with the same monitor and measures. Between 6 and 33\% of their alarms fall near a scored boundary, and they catch 1 to 8 episodes under the direction-aware check. The two best cells, the Fourier regression and TBATS with EWMA, catch 8, one more than the best past-only monitors (Section~6.5).

\paragraph{Past-only against retrospective fits.} The two model pairs point in opposite directions. Retrospective MSTL catches 6 episodes with EWMA or FOCuS, against 4 and 3 for MSTL refitted on the past, which needs two years for a yearly pattern. For inserted 40\% drops the regression refitted on the past gained 0.11 to 0.38 with the four uncapped detectors. The same model fitted to the whole series gained only 0.05 to 0.22. Its flexible trend bends towards an inserted step and absorbs part of it, so a retrospective fit helps only if it keeps a change separate from its trend.

\paragraph{Offline segmentation.} PELT, Binary Segmentation and WBS on the retrospective STL and MSTL series, with their usual penalties, marked 30 to 98 change dates in six and a half years and caught 7 to 14 episodes under the main rule and 4 to 8 under the direction-aware check, against 12 and 9 for the best live monitor, but with a change every three to eleven weeks. On the retrospective regression they marked 58 to 73 change dates and caught 10 to 12 episodes under the main rule and 6 or 7 under the direction-aware check.

\section{Scoring-rule variants and robustness}\label{app:rules}

Table~B.1 scores the leading monitors under every version of the scoring rule. Section~4.6.1 tells how the rule changed. Variant 3 is the first step taken on 23 October 2026, and Variants 1 and 2 change one condition at a time.

\paragraph{What each version adds.} For the same-weekday difference with CUSUM, of the six catches the nearest-boundary version adds over the direction-aware check, two are echoes in the 2025 industrial action and four are alarms two to four days before a dated order or announced strike, when travel was already changing. The main rule adds one more, an alarm that counts for two close boundaries. So this monitor falls from 12 to 5 as the echoes, the alarms before a boundary and the double credits are removed. Holt-Winters with EWMA catches 8 under the main rule and 7 under every other version, because its alarms come after the boundary and in its direction. What changes most is the chance level. A wider window or no condition on direction lets chance catch more, so the best of 137 shifted alarm lists reaches 7 in 11 to 96\% of shifts under the other versions, and in only 0.6\% under the direction-aware check. This is why Holt-Winters with EWMA has a steady count but not a steady $p$-value. After the adjustment its $p$ is 0.78 under the main rule, 0.82, 0.11 and 0.14 under the three variants, and 0.006 under the direction-aware check.

\paragraph{Holding under both rules.} Requiring a monitor to hold under both rules is no stricter than the direction-aware check alone. In every one of 2{,}000 circular and 2{,}000 era shifts, chance's best count under both rules equalled its best direction-aware count. So the test is the direction-aware one, with the same adjusted $p$ for a count of 7: 0.006 with circular shifts and 0.0045 with era shifts. Five monitors reach 7 under both rules: the yearly memory with CUSUM (9, $p$ (adjusted, both rules) $<0.001$) and the median of four with EWMA (8, 0.002), both with the independent threshold; Holt-Winters with EWMA (7, 0.006); the yearly memory with FOCuS and the independent threshold (7, 0.006); and retrospective STL with FOCuS-AR (7, 0.006).

\paragraph{Robustness.} The chance verdicts do not depend on how the alarms are shifted. Shifting each era's alarms only within 2020--22 or within 2023--26 gives the same picture for the best of 137: 12 under the main rule has $p$ (adjusted) $=0.011$ and 7 under the direction-aware check 0.005. Without the Omicron episode, which the frozen list marks as confounded with Christmas, Holt-Winters with EWMA catches 6 of 14, which the best of 137 reaches in only 2\% of shifts. It also beats chance on both the 9 announced and the 6 unannounced episodes ($p$ (own) $=0.007$ and $0.003$). Without the COVID-19 onset each count falls by at most one, and the best of 137 still survives under the main rule (11, $p$ (adjusted) $=0.01$) and the direction-aware check (8, $<0.001$). The one exception is the fixed monitor with CUSUM, whose 3 episodes become 2 of 14 ($p$ (own) $=0.16$). One scored boundary, 15 January 2025, is also a date the publisher flagged for data quality. Without it three of the five monitors that hold under both rules lose one episode, but all five still hold, the yearly memory with CUSUM and the independent threshold at $p$ (adjusted, both rules) $=0.002$, and with a lower bar 17 monitors hold (files \texttt{no\_dq\_2025\_01.csv} and \texttt{familywise\_no\_dq.csv} in the repository).

\paragraph{Shares of variation on Opal.} We split the variation in non-event alarms across the six past-only forecasts with CUSUM, EWMA and FOCuS and both threshold rules. The threshold rule explains 45\% and the forecast 11\%. With the capped detectors and CPM added, the detector explains 52\%, almost all of it capped or rank-based against the rest. For episodes caught under the main rule, the forecast also leads with only the four main forecasts, at 78\% against 10\% for the detector. Under the direction-aware check, with all six past-only forecasts the forecast explains 64\% and the detector 8\%, with intervals of 20 to 88\% and 2 to 19\%. With only the four main forecasts, which leaves out the two refitted ones that catch least, the split is 12\% and 31\%, and the forecast explains more in only 59\% of resamples.
